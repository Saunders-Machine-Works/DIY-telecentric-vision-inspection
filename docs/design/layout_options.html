Vision rig: one printed body, much shorter

Today's rig is about 700 mm (27.7 in) long and several separate prints. Below are three ways to fold it into one printed structure, drawn at the same scale, with what each costs you. I recommend the tower, after a cheap mirror test.

Where the 700 mm goes

Everything on the right of the part is fixed by the lens: the part must sit exactly 158 mm from the lens front (depth of field is only 32 mm), and the lens and camera are rigid lengths. Only the light side on the left can be folded freely.

Option A · keeps the imaging path untouched

A1. Light folded beside the camera path

Two small first-surface mirrors (about 40 × 75 mm, roughly $20 each) turn the light around the end of the part station, so the tunnel and backlight run alongside the focal distance instead of behind the part. The lens and camera stay in a straight line. Parts still stand on a pedestal.

  1. 1Reticle gate: a slit in the focal plane, through the part's axis, so the chessboard reticle drops in and out without moving anything.
  2. 2Centring screws: fine screws move the part holder and the slot plate sideways about ±3 mm, then lock. The app already reports the light balance, so you adjust until it reads near zero.
  3. 3Mirror mounts with two tilt screws each; the light cone is ±3.4°, so a degree of error is harmless.
  4. 4Lens saddles: two printed cradles with straps, so the lens carries its own weight and the camera is not hanging off the C-mount.
  5. 5Camera cradle with cable strain relief.
  6. 6Electronics bay for the backlight controller and power supply, with cable channels in the base.

Option A · your idea, tunnel under the camera path

A2. Light folded below the camera path

The same two-mirror fold, stacked under the lens instead of beside it. The base gets narrower, and the plan footprint drops a little. Because the light stack now runs underneath the part, a pedestal cannot stand there. The part has to hang from a top chuck on an arm that reaches over the focal distance, and the beam is 70 mm wide in this view, so the mirrors grow to about 100 × 40 mm.

Option B · smallest footprint

B. Tower: the camera path folded straight up

One 45° first-surface mirror, about 100 × 140 mm, turns the imaging path upward. The lens and camera stand vertically beside the part, and the tunnel and backlight stay straight behind it. The part stands on a pedestal as it does now. This is the only layout that shrinks the lens side, which is the long side.

Recommendation

Take B for the footprint you asked for, but only after a one-hour mirror test on today's rig. Put a 100 mm first-surface mirror at 45° in the focal distance and measure the 0.5009 in pin and the reticle. If the pin reads within 0.00005 in of today's value and the calibration residual does not change, the tower is safe. If not, build A1, which cannot touch the measurement path.

A2 is not worth it: it is barely smaller than today in plan, it forces hung parts, and it needs bigger mirrors than A1.

Folding the camera path sideways with two mirrors does not fit. Two mirrors need at least 178 mm of focal distance to clear the tunnel; the lens has 158 mm.

One printed body

  • Material and size. PETG on the H2D, each section no more than about 300 mm, with 3–4 mm walls and a stiffening grid where the lens and camera sit.
  • Joints. Sections join with a tongue-and-groove or dovetail, two steel dowel pins for alignment and M4 bolts into heat-set inserts. The joints sit where nothing optical crosses them.
  • Light-tight. Black PETG, flocked inside the tunnel and the shroud, with a lid over the part station and an open top for tall parts.
  • Print layout. B prints as a base with the light stack in two sections, and a column in three stacked sections. A1 prints as a base in two sections, plus the lid and mirror mounts.

Vertical or horizontal parts: swap in two minutes

The slot plate is a square cassette that fits in two orientations. Turning it 90° puts the narrow 24 mm side across a horizontal part's diameter, which is what the taped window did on the bench. The part holder is a plug on a dovetail seat: a pedestal for vertical parts, or a pair of V-blocks for horizontal ones.

What I need to approve this and start the CAD

  1. Which layout? My pick is B after the mirror test, with A1 as the fallback. If you would rather skip the test and build A1 now, say so.
  2. Real part sizes. The CAD depends on the actual outlines. Please give me, or photograph with a ruler: the backlight (4 × 4 in? thickness?), the DCS-100E controller and power supply, the reticle plate, and the camera with its connectors.
  3. Bench limits. The largest footprint and height that fit where this will live.
  4. Part limits. How tall a part can stand above the measurement zone, and the largest diameter you will hold. The field of view is 70 mm; the slot is 24 mm wide.
